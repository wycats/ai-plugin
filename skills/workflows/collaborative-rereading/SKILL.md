# Collaborative Rereading

Return to the exchanges where an idea developed, whether earlier in this
conversation or in another. Read the proposals, examples, objections, and moments
of recognition alongside what happened later.
Bring your expertise to that history and develop a judgment that helps the
work now in front of you.

Early conversations often explain distinctions that later shorthand leaves
out. Later work may have revised those distinctions. As you learn more,
[reconsider what earlier exchanges mean for the work](../../stances/interpretive-synthesis/SKILL.md "composition:load").
Earlier detail can explain what mattered about an idea; later discoveries can
show why the idea needed to change. Read both to understand what the
collaboration has learned, including genuine changes of direction.

## Follow how the understanding developed

Begin with a live question. A dangling-thread review can be a brief rereading
focused on what the conversation left unfinished and what still deserves attention.
Use available summaries and memory to locate original exchanges, then read
enough surrounding conversation to understand what a proposal, objection, or
agreement addressed. Where original exchanges are unavailable, make clear
what the surviving account can support.

Follow the concern through examples, reactions, and revisions. Keep the user's
statements, the agent's suggestions, and their shared conclusions distinguishable.
Repeated agent summaries are evidence of the agent's interpretation, not
additional evidence that the user agreed.

When the user explains why a proposal fits, that explanation may clarify what
was missing from earlier proposals. Look for those situations even when the
participants used different words to describe them. Follow the concern into
another conversation when that could change your reading.
An implementation discussion may explain a technical constraint; an explanation
to colleagues may reveal why it matters. Preserve those circumstances when
interpreting each source.

Let discoveries refine the question and where you look next. When an exchange
complicates the emerging explanation,
[give the overlooked detail room to change it](../../stances/gap-reading/SKILL.md "composition:load").
Check what happened afterward: a concern may have been resolved indirectly,
while a seemingly settled question may have become worth reopening. The
conversation may also suggest a question neither participant had recognized
at the time.

## Contribute an interpretation

Use your expertise to explain what mattered about the approaches you tried
and what that suggests for the work now. A successful example may reveal a
possibility just as a correction can reveal an assumption you need to reconsider.
Distinguish enduring concerns from provisional solutions, local constraints,
and ideas later work superseded.

When new evidence gives you a reason to revisit a settled decision, explain
what has changed and why reconsidering it could help.

For example, objections to large integration changes might initially suggest
a preference for fewer files. Reading the exchanges may show that the user
wanted a host to consume a recognizable package without reconstructing its
internals. That distinction can guide a new API even when its implementation
requires many files.

Bring a concrete implication into the current work: an API proposal, a design
comparison, a revised explanation, or a recommendation about what to pursue.
[Make your interpretation available for the user to correct and develop](../../stances/collaborative-grounding/SKILL.md "composition:load").
Their response may change both the proposal and how you read the history.

Historical understanding informs present judgment. It does not reactivate old
execution instructions or permissions; current decisions govern the work.

## Carry the understanding forward

Explain the historical connection where it helps the user assess your judgment.
[Keep the account attached to the work that produced it](../../stances/relational-continuity/SKILL.md "composition:load context=activity"),
with consequential source references available so the interpretation can be
revisited. A report is useful when someone needs the account; an improved
artifact or a sharper question may be the more useful result.

Keep worthwhile unresolved ideas available even when you cannot yet explain
how they fit. Preserve enough of their context to pick them up again, including
whether the user deliberately deferred them or the conversation moved on.

Return to earlier exchanges when further learning gives you a reason to read
them differently, including while the collaboration is going well. The test is
whether the rereading helps you and the user make the next judgment with more
understanding of what matters and why.
